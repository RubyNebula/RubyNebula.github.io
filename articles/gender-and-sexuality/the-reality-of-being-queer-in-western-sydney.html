The Reality of Being Queer in Western Sydney

This is my personal story of homophobia and survival.

Home Writings

Lurking in the cold shadows of Western Sydney’s alleys is a darkness derived from hatred.

It was only three years ago that I finally graduated high school. The ghosts of the memories formed there still haunt me, even as I have now escaped the “enclaves” marked by a massive immigrant and Muslim population.

It is a darkness that makes gay and trans children hide. The fear does not come from a monster underneath their bed, but for fear of their next-door neighbor.

Unfortunately, I was raised and born in a community brimming with religious fundamentalism and rigid ideological conformity to the Quran. Like a heavy blanket, homophobia, misogyny and transphobia suffocated the surrounding landscape.

Nowhere was this more clear to me than the prison-like classrooms of Birrong Girls High School. For bright students, for ambitious students, for diverse students, for queer students, high school is the epitome of hell.

And hell is made all the worse when you’re a 17-year-old girl who just discovered she has a crush on another girl.

My sexuality was more surprising to myself than anyone else. I did not choose to fall for the girl who sat next to me in business studies class. One day she walked into the library, and I just happened to feel butterflies in my stomach.

Excited, as all teenagers are when they form a new crush, I told some people who I thought were trustworthy. Over the weekend, I felt ecstatic and on the verge of new horizons.

When I returned to school on the Monday, I found out that everything had gone to shit. Rumors had spread. I was branded as disgusting, outcasted and ostracized for an immutable characteristic I had no control over. My new-found love quickly turned sour. The irony was that she had same-sex attractions for a girl herself; the internalized homophobia was obvious. Even more ironic was how most queer peers commented on her “gay vibes” and asked me if she was truly straight.

The teachers reinforced the pattern of homophobia, as most teachers were practicing Muslims. During history class, a teacher asked each student which cause they were most passionate about. One student almost jumped out of her seat as she went on and on about how the LGBT community is a mental illness. The teacher’s response was simply nothing.

She stood there and did not intervene. Looking back on it now, I wondered if she felt the tension between her loyalty to the Quran and her duties as a teacher to protect students from bigotry and ensure a safe learning environment.

I felt abandoned and hurt. But, an inner spark burned in me. I had a passion and voice. I confided in some other queer students at my school, who warned me about the consequences of acting upon my inner spark. They told me people would become aggressive. But if I backed down, then I would essentially be allowing the bullies and bigots to win, so I refused to do that.

Slowly, over the months of my senior year of high school, I painted on five canvases. I changed and redid my paintings multiple times. Until one day, when the gates were still open at the end of school, I sneaked in and presented my paintings to the school counselor. The school counselor displayed my paintings in her office. In a feat of bravery, I decided to honor my work, my courage and my bravery in the face of potential backlash by scrawling my name onto the paintings.

Later, I found out that the school counselor had to remove my paintings, hiding them away in the back of the room with the painting covered, because it disturbed students.

Yes, you heard that correctly! The depiction of diverse forms of love disturbed students.

I survived through admirable courage, authenticity and acceptance. In those years, I refused to abandon myself. As I entered my adult years, I learned to strip away the shame. I celebrated my sexuality at queer parties and went to my first gay parade.

It was a burst of colors, laughter and people of all backgrounds and stories. Together, they danced and smiled through the parade in a show of glitter, sparkle and light. The energy was as electric as lightning. From all over the city, people gathered to celebrate love.

It’s unfortunate to see such a lack of queer spaces and night scenes in Muslim neighborhoods. It’s as if there is a stark boundary line between the rest of the city and the “enclaves.” Accommodating the queer community in Muslim communities through nightclubs, bars, specialized peer support groups, pride celebrations, LGBT history seminars, and queer community events would have helped young people like me who were coming of age and discovering their sexuality.

One day, I would like to walk the suburbs where I grew up and see Pride flags hanging brightly from balconies and windows. I would love to see queer art adorn the walls of schools and buildings without fear of angry students knocking them down because they feel uncomfortable.

A young girl of seventeen like me did not deserve the homophobia I experienced. I deserved safety, compassion and love. In the future, people just like me deserve to be embraced and welcomed when coming out about their sexuality. They deserve to know that school administrations will protect them and silence bigotry in the classrooms. They deserve to feel in their bodies that they will be treated like a human being regardless of how they love others.
